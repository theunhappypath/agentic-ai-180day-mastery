The first thing anyone notices about the old library is how quiet it is, and the
second thing is that the quiet is not empty. There is the hum of the radiator in
winter, the soft complaint of a chair being pushed back, the sound of someone
turning a page two tables over. People come here to be alone in company, which is
a particular kind of comfort that is hard to arrange anywhere else.

Martha has worked at the front desk for nineteen years. She knows which of the
regulars will ask for help and which would rather wander until they find what
they were not looking for. She keeps a small notebook under the counter where she
writes down the questions she could not answer, and once a month she works
through the list on a slow afternoon. Most of them turn out to be simple. A few
of them have been in the notebook for years.

The building itself was never meant to be a library. It went up in 1908 as a
grain exchange, and if you stand in the reading room and look at the ceiling you
can still see the iron beams that once held a hoist. The town bought it for very
little money in 1954, after the exchange moved to the county seat and the
building sat empty long enough for the roof to fail in two places. The renovation
took four years and ran over budget, which is the sort of detail that gets
argued about at council meetings and then forgotten entirely by everyone who
actually uses the place.

What surprises visitors is how much of the work is not about books. There is a
printer that people rely on for job applications. There is a room in the back
where a volunteer teaches basic arithmetic to adults who never had the chance to
learn it properly, and who arrive with a specific kind of embarrassment that
takes about three weeks to wear off. There is a shelf near the door for seed
packets, which someone started as a joke in 2011 and which now has a waiting
list every spring. None of this was planned. It accumulated, the way useful
things usually do, because somebody noticed a need and nobody said no.

The children's section is at the far end, deliberately, so that noise from it
does not carry. On Saturday mornings it is loud in a way that would horrify the
people who designed the original building, and Martha has never once asked
anyone there to be quieter. Her theory is that a child who learns a library is a
place where you have to whisper will remember it as a place where you were told
off, and will not come back at twenty-five when they need the printer.

Funding is reviewed every three years. Each time, someone points out that
circulation is down, which is true, and that most people have the internet at
home, which is also true, and then someone else stands up and says something
about the arithmetic class or the seed shelf, and the funding continues. This has
happened five times now. Martha has stopped worrying about it, mostly. She keeps
the notebook, she answers what she can, and in the evening she turns off the
lamps at the long tables one by one, walking the length of the room in the dark
because after nineteen years she knows exactly where everything is.
